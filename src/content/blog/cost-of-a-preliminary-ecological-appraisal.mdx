If you've been told you need a Preliminary Ecological Appraisal before your planning application will be validated, your first question is almost certainly going to be about the cost. It's a fair question, and the honest answer is that it varies more than most people expect.

This is a plain-English look at what a PEA costs in the UK in 2026, what pushes the price up or down, and what you should actually be getting for the money. No sales pitch, just the numbers as we see them.

## The short answer on PEA cost

For a small, straightforward site — a single dwelling plot, a modest extension, a paddock — most PEA quotes land somewhere in the region of £400 to £1,500. For larger or more complicated sites, you can comfortably be looking at several thousand pounds.

Here's a rough guide, with the usual caveat that these are typical ranges and not fixed prices:

| Site type | Typical PEA fee (2026) |
| --- | --- |
| Small, simple site (single plot, garden, paddock) | £400–£900 |
| Medium site, some habitat complexity | £900–£1,800 |
| Large or complex site, multiple habitats | £2,000–£5,000+ |

Treat that table as a starting point for conversations, not a quote. Two consultants looking at the same field can come back with quite different figures, and both can be perfectly reasonable.

## What you're actually paying for

A PEA isn't just a site visit. The work breaks down into three parts:

- **A desk study.** The ecologist pulls records from the local environmental records centre, checks designated sites nearby, and looks at historical mapping and aerial imagery to understand what's likely to be present.
- **An Extended Phase 1 Habitat Survey** — increasingly done to the UKHab classification — which is the walkover where they map habitats on the ground and note features that could support protected species.
- **A written report** that pulls it together, sets out the constraints, and recommends next steps, usually so a planning officer can sign off the ecology side of your application.

If you want the full picture of what lands on your desk at the end, we've written separately about [what a PEA actually contains](/blog/what-a-pea-actually-contains). The point worth making here is that the report is the deliverable. The site visit is the easy bit; the desk study and the writing are where the time goes, and that's reflected in the fee.

## What drives the price up or down

A handful of factors do most of the work in setting your quote.

**Site size and complexity.** A back garden takes an hour to walk. A 40-hectare mixed parcel with woodland, watercourses, and old farm buildings takes a day or more, plus far longer to write up. Size and habitat variety are the biggest single lever on cost.

**Travel and region.** Ecologists charge for getting to you. A site an hour from the consultant's base is cheaper to survey than one three hours away, and regional rates differ — work in and around the South East tends to cost more than the same job in, say, rural Lancashire.

**Survey season.** The habitat walkover is most reliable in the optimal botanical window, broadly April to September. Out-of-season surveys are possible, but the ecologist may need to caveat their findings or budget for a return visit, both of which can nudge the price and the timeline.

**Protected-species risk.** If the walkover flags potential for bats, great crested newts, badgers, or other protected species, the report will recommend further surveys. The PEA itself doesn't get more expensive for finding these things, but it sets off a chain of follow-on work that does.

**Turnaround.** A standard lead time is built into most quotes. Ask for a report next week rather than next month and you may pay a premium, if the consultant can fit it in at all.

## The cost that isn't in the PEA quote

This is the part that catches people out. A PEA frequently recommends further surveys, and those are separate jobs with their own fees and their own seasonal windows.

Bat emergence surveys, for instance, can only happen between roughly May and September and often need multiple visits. Great crested newt surveys are similarly tied to the spring. If your PEA triggers one of these, the cost and the delay can dwarf the appraisal itself — and you can't simply pay to skip the season.

So when you're budgeting, treat the PEA fee as the entry ticket rather than the full bill. A good consultant will tell you up front how likely follow-on surveys are for your site, which lets you plan around the calendar instead of being ambushed by it.

## Why cheaper isn't always cheaper

It's tempting to take the lowest quote and move on. Sometimes that's the right call. But a thin report that a planning officer queries can cost you weeks, and weeks of delay on a development are worth a great deal more than the difference between two survey fees.

There's also a wider squeeze on the market worth being aware of. The arrival of mandatory Biodiversity Net Gain has piled work onto a finite number of ecologists, and we've written about how [BNG is burying small ecological consultancies](/blog/bng-is-burying-ecological-consultancies) — which is part of why good consultants are busy and lead times have lengthened. It's a seller's market in places, and that shows up in both price and availability.

What you want to check, regardless of price, is that the consultant is properly qualified — CIEEM membership is the usual marker — and that the quote covers a genuine desk study and a competent report, not just a flying visit and a template.

## Getting a sensible quote

A few practical steps will get you a number you can trust:

- Send a site location and a red-line boundary, even a rough one. It's the single most useful thing for accurate pricing.
- Say when you need the report. Deadlines change what's possible and what it costs.
- Ask directly how likely follow-on surveys are, so you can budget for the whole job rather than just the first step.
- Get more than one quote, and compare what's included, not only the headline figure.
