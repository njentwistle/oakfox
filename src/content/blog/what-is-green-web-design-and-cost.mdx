"Green web design" reads like a style — recycled-paper textures, leaf motifs, a muted palette. It isn't. It's a build discipline: a set of decisions about images, code, fonts, and hosting that determine how much energy a website burns every time someone opens it. A green site can look like anything. What it can't do is waste.

## What it actually involves

The discipline, in rough order of impact — the same order our audit data supports:

**An image pipeline, not just images.** Photography is [60% of everything the average small-business page transfers](/blog/we-audited-100-uk-small-business-websites). Green design means every image is right-sized for its slot, served in modern formats (WebP, AVIF), and lazy-loaded below the fold — automatically, as part of the build, not as a favour someone remembers occasionally.

**Static-first architecture.** Most marketing sites don't need an application framework rendering every visit. Static-first builds — the approach behind [our practical guide to sustainable web design](/blog/sustainable-web-design-practical-guide) — ship pages as plain files: minimal JavaScript, nothing computed twice, nothing loaded that isn't used.

**Font restraint.** Two families, subsetted, or the system stack. Web fonts were 10% of all bytes in our study, and 43 of 99 sites shipped over 200 KB of them — mostly character sets that never render.

**Nothing that plays itself.** Autoplaying video backgrounds are the single most expensive design habit per second of attention they buy.

**Verified hosting underneath.** The page determines the bytes; [the hosting determines the emissions per byte](/blog/carbon-neutral-vs-green-web-hosting). Green design finishes the job on infrastructure listed in The Green Web Foundation's directory.

**Measurement, before and after.** A grams-per-view figure with a date on it, from [a carbon audit](/website-carbon-audit) — because "we build green" without a number is exactly the kind of claim [UK regulators now expect businesses to substantiate](/blog/sustainability-claims-without-greenwashing).

## What it doesn't mean

It doesn't mean plain. When we [audited businesses that sell sustainability](/blog/websites-of-businesses-that-sell-sustainability), the A-rated sites looked considered, not austere — modest imagery well handled reads as confidence. What separates an A from an F isn't ambition; it's whether anyone managed the weight. The F-rated sites weren't more beautiful, they were just heavier — which, on a phone on 4G, mostly means slower.

## What it costs

Honest UK numbers, using our own as the example: web design engagements at OakFox run from around **£3,500** for a focused landing page to **£15,000+** for a full multi-page site with a design system and accessibility auditing — and the green discipline is part of the craft, not a premium line item. That's the general shape of the market too: you're paying for design and build quality, and sustainability done properly is a *way of doing* that work, not an extra service bolted on top.

Where green design actually moves money:

- **Running costs go down.** Lighter pages need less bandwidth and more modest hosting — [verified green plans start at £9 a month](/services/green-web-hosting), the same territory as conventional shared hosting.
- **Performance pays commercially.** The carbon work and the speed work are the same work; speed feeds Core Web Vitals, rankings, and conversion. You'd buy this outcome anyway — the sustainability claim comes free with it.
- **The claim is usable.** "Our site runs on verified renewable hosting and emits under X grams per view, tested on this date" is specific, checkable marketing copy — the kind that survives scrutiny.

If you're commissioning a site and want the discipline built in, the brief is simple: ask for a page-weight budget, a grams-per-view target, and the test date in the handover pack. Any studio that flinches at those three requests has answered your real question.
