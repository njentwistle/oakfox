Naming is the single hardest decision in a new brand. It is the one thing almost impossible to change cleanly once momentum builds, and the one thing everyone in the room has an opinion on. This is the process we use at OakFox — refined over 15 years of [brand identity projects](/services/branding) — to turn naming from a fraught democratic exercise into a structured decision.

## What a Good Brand Name Has to Do

A brand name is not a poem. It is a working asset that has to:

1. **Be memorable** — pronounceable, spellable, and reducible to a single clear idea.
2. **Be legally available** — trademarkable in your jurisdiction, with a useable `.com` or relevant domain.
3. **Survive internationalisation** — not mean something unfortunate in a language you expect customers to speak.
4. **Fit the positioning** — a luxury wine merchant needs a different name from a low-cost coffee chain.
5. **Age well** — not be so on-trend that it dates in three years.
6. **Be flexible** — leave room for the brand to evolve without rebrand paralysis.

The best names rarely excel at all six. Most nail three or four and hold their own on the rest.

## The Process

### Stage 1 — Define the constraints (1 hour)

Before generating a single candidate, write down:

- **What the brand is and does** — one paragraph, same as you would put in a [branding brief](/blog/how-to-brief-a-branding-agency).
- **Who it is for** — primary audience, language, cultural context.
- **Positioning** — premium, accessible, disruptive, heritage, etc.
- **Tonal boundaries** — should it be warm or cold? Serious or playful? Traditional or modern?
- **Phonetic preferences** — hard consonants (commanding, memorable: Nike, Kodak) or soft consonants (warmer, more approachable: Aesop, Warby Parker)? Short (2 syllables, punchy) or longer (more substantive)?
- **Avoid list** — sectors, competitors, archetypes you do not want to echo.

Writing this down forces clarity and gives you a filter to judge candidates against. If you skip it, you will debate naming for weeks in circles.

### Stage 2 — Generate a longlist (4–6 hours)

Aim for 80–120 candidate names. You need volume to find the good ones. Use multiple naming strategies in parallel:

- **Descriptive** — directly describes what the business does (BookingCom, General Motors, The Body Shop). Easy to understand, hard to trademark, limited flexibility.
- **Evocative** — suggests an idea or feeling without being literal (Patagonia, Tesla, Amazon). Stronger trademarks, more memorable, need more marketing to explain.
- **Invented** — made-up words (Kodak, Xerox, Google). Best trademarks, highest marketing cost, biggest upside.
- **Founder-based** — uses a person's name (Jo Malone, Aesop Rock, Warren James). Works for certain sectors, harder to sell the business later.
- **Compound** — two existing words fused (Facebook, SnapChat, OakFox). Middle ground — memorable, reasonably trademarkable.
- **Metaphorical** — borrowed from nature, mythology, or culture (Nike after the Greek goddess, Apple for simplicity). Works well when the metaphor is apt.

Do not judge at this stage. Get to 80. A good exercise: ban editing for the first hour and just write.

### Stage 3 — Cull to a shortlist (2–3 hours)

Now judge. Against your Stage 1 constraints, remove anything that:

- Is actively bad when read aloud three times
- Requires explanation every time it is said
- Echoes a competitor too closely
- Has a cringe-inducing connotation
- Is obviously hard to spell or pronounce

You want to land on 15–20 candidates.

### Stage 4 — Legal and commercial checks (half a day)

For each shortlisted candidate:

- **UK IPO trademark search** — [ipo.gov.uk/tmcase](https://www.gov.uk/search-for-trademark). Free. Check classes relevant to your business.
- **EUIPO search** if you expect to trade in the EU — [euipo.europa.eu/eSearch/](https://euipo.europa.eu/eSearch/).
- **Companies House search** — [gov.uk/get-information-about-a-company](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/). Not a deal-breaker if someone shares a name in an unrelated sector, but worth knowing.
- **Google the name** — existing businesses, news stories, anything awkward on the first page.
- **Domain availability** — `.com` ideal, `.co.uk` acceptable for UK-first brands, `.shop` / `.studio` / `.design` niche.
- **Social handle availability** — check Instagram, LinkedIn, and the platforms your sector uses. Use [namecheckr.com](https://www.namecheckr.com/) as a quick starter.
- **International meaning check** — drop candidates into Google Translate across five or six languages. An uncomfortable number of startup names mean something inappropriate in German, Portuguese, or Swedish.

This stage typically kills half your shortlist. That is the point.

### Stage 5 — Stress test the survivors (1 hour per candidate)

For the 5–7 candidates that pass legal and commercial checks, stress-test each:

- **The radio test** — can a friend understand it over a bad phone line and spell it correctly?
- **The ten-year test** — will this name still feel right in 2036?
- **The positioning test** — if you described your brand as the name out of context, what would a stranger guess the business sells? Is it close to what you actually do?
- **The logo test** — sketch a logotype in three different weights. Does it hold together visually? Short punchy names win here; long ones struggle.
- **The "what's it called?" test** — after a customer tells a friend about you, what will they remember of the name? First syllable? Shape of the word? Misremembered variant?

### Stage 6 — Choose, and commit

The final step is the hardest: pick one and commit. Do not let the decision drag past a week. Ambiguity compounds — every day you do not commit is a day you cannot build the website, register the company, order signage, or brief a designer.

If two candidates are genuinely tied, pick the one that is shorter, easier to trademark, and less likely to be confused with a competitor. That is almost always the right call.

## Worked Examples

### Aigéan

When we named [Aigéan](/portfolio/aigean), the Irish seafood restaurant, the client wanted something that communicated Irish provenance without being a cliché. "Aigéan" is the Irish word for "ocean" — pronounced roughly "AG-awn". Chosen because:

- Direct connection to the business (seafood, ocean)
- Distinctive in a Dublin market full of English-language names
- Trademarkable as a non-obvious word in food services
- Short, two syllables, holds together in a logotype
- Unpronounceable on first sight but becomes a gentle education for diners — part of the brand story

Tradeoffs accepted: customers will mispronounce it the first time. The team now treat every pronunciation correction as an opening to talk about the menu.

### Roll

For [Roll](/portfolio/roll), the Lake District bakery and coffee shop, we went the opposite direction:

- A single-syllable word
- Directly evocative of the product (bread rolls, coffee rolls, roll of ground coffee)
- Simple logotype potential
- Trademarkable in hospitality with specific application
- Internationally universal — the word reads similarly across English-speaking markets

Tradeoffs accepted: "Roll" on its own is undifferentiated in search. We specified "Roll Bakery" in the extended word mark and anchored search listings to the town name ("Roll, Bowness") for local discovery.

### CubTrack

For [CubTrack](/portfolio/cubtrack), the baby and toddler app, the name had to do immediate functional work:

- "Cub" — universally understood reference to young mammals, warm connotation
- "Track" — direct signal of the app's function (tracking feeding, sleep, development)
- Compound word — strong trademark potential in the app store category
- Short, easy to search, obvious domain fit
- Works across iOS and Android store listings at first glance

Tradeoffs accepted: some parents find "cub" overly cute. We addressed this in the brand voice — spare, helpful, practical — so the product feel balances the name.

## Common Mistakes

- **Naming by committee**. Five people will never agree on a single name. Two people who trust each other can.
- **Falling in love with the first idea you liked**. The first name that feels right is usually the one you are emotionally attached to, not the best candidate. Kill it deliberately and see what survives.
- **Picking a name you cannot buy the `.com` for**. Exceptions exist (Warby Parker is `warbyparker.com`, Canva is `canva.com` — both exact match) but in 2026 the cost of failing to secure a strong domain compounds. If the exact `.com` is taken by a parked page, it is almost always worth paying the four-figure asking price for a viable brand.
- **Trademarking after launching**. Launch with a name, spend six months building equity, then discover the trademark is unavailable — it happens, and the rebrand costs far more than the £170 UK IPO application you should have filed at the start.
- **Ignoring pronunciation**. "Aigéan" is intentional ambiguity. A badly-chosen unpronounceable name is just a barrier to recommendation.
- **Choosing a name that boxes you in**. A name that is too literal constrains future pivots. If "Corner Coffee" decides to sell tea, the brand is broken.
