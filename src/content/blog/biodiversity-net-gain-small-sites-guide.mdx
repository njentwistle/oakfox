If you're building anything small in England right now — a few houses, a modest commercial unit, an infill plot — Biodiversity Net Gain applies to you, and the rules weren't really written with you in mind. They were written for major schemes with ecologists on retainer and a planning budget to match.

This is a plain-English run through what BNG actually asks of a small or minor development, where the real costs sit, and how to keep both the spend and the paperwork sane. None of it is legal advice, and thresholds shift, so treat this as a map rather than the territory.

## What BNG requires, in one paragraph

Biodiversity Net Gain has been mandatory for major developments in England since February 2024, and for small sites since April. The core requirement is the same whatever your size: your development has to leave biodiversity in a measurably better state, by a minimum of **10%**, and that gain has to be secured and maintained for at least **30 years**. "Measurably" is the operative word — it's not a judgement call, it's a calculation.

## How the 10% is measured

The uplift is worked out using the **Statutory Biodiversity Metric**, a spreadsheet tool that scores habitats by type, condition and area before and after development. Subtract the before from the after, and you need to land at least 10% in the black.

For smaller schemes there's a lighter option:

- **The Small Sites Metric (SSM)** — a simplified version of the statutory metric, designed to be less demanding to complete on eligible small sites.
- It won't suit every plot. Sites with protected or priority habitats, or where protected species are involved, usually fall outside it and need the full metric.
- An ecologist can tell you within a site visit or two which one you're allowed to use, which is worth knowing before you price anything.

The metric rewards condition, not just area. A scrappy strip of amenity grass scores poorly; the same strip turned into a properly managed wildflower meadow scores well. That's the lever you're pulling.

## Are you a small site — and are you exempt?

"Small site" has a specific planning meaning, and it's worth getting right because it decides which metric and which process you're in. The general thresholds are:

- **Residential:** 1 to 9 dwellings, on a site under 0.5 hectares.
- **Commercial:** floor space under 1,000 square metres, or a site area under 1 hectare.

Treat those as the usual cut-off rather than gospel. The precise wording can change, and your Local Planning Authority is the one who decides, so confirm your exact case with them before you build a strategy around it.

Some developments are exempt altogether. Certain householder applications, self-build and custom-build, and various de minimis cases below the relevant threshold can sit outside BNG. The list isn't fixed and the detail matters, so check the current gov.uk guidance and ask your LPA rather than assuming yours qualifies. An exemption you can't actually rely on is worse than none, because you'll find out at validation.

## The hierarchy: where the money actually goes

There's an order you're meant to follow, and conveniently the cheapest options usually come first:

- **Avoid and minimise** habitat loss on site. Keep the mature trees, the hedgerow, the pond. Every unit you don't destroy is a unit you don't have to replace.
- **Enhance on site.** Improve what's left, or create new habitat within the red line. This is normally the cheapest route to your 10%.
- **Buy off-site units** if you can't get there on site. You're paying a landowner or habitat bank to deliver the gain elsewhere.
- **Statutory credits** from the government, as a genuine last resort. They're priced to be unattractive on purpose, to push you up the hierarchy.

The practical takeaway: design biodiversity in early, while the layout is still soft. Retrofitting a few percent of net gain onto a scheme that's already been laid out for maximum plot count is where small developers end up reaching for expensive off-site units they didn't need.

## The 30-year commitment everyone underestimates

The bit that catches small developers out isn't the metric or the 10% — it's the **30-year management and monitoring obligation** that comes attached to any gain you create. Whatever habitat you promise has to be looked after, monitored and reported on for three decades, secured through a planning condition, a section 106 agreement or a conservation covenant.

That's a real, long-tailed liability. The planting is cheap; thirty years of mowing regimes, condition assessments, monitoring reports and the legal mechanism behind them is not. On a one-off small site it can quietly outweigh the build cost of the habitat itself. Sometimes that maths is exactly why an off-site unit — where someone else carries the 30-year burden — turns out to be the sensible choice after all. Run the numbers properly before you assume on-site is cheaper across its whole life.

## Keeping it sane: a short checklist

- **Get an ecologist in before the layout is fixed**, not after. Early advice is cheap; redesigns aren't.
- **Confirm your thresholds and exemptions with your LPA in writing.** Don't rely on a forum post or a half-remembered rule.
- **Price the full 30 years**, not just the planting, when you compare on-site against off-site.
- **Check SSM eligibility early** — it can meaningfully cut the assessment burden if you qualify.
- **Keep your habitat evidence tidy** from day one, because the metric is only as good as the baseline survey behind it.

BNG on a small site is manageable. It's mostly a matter of sequencing — doing the ecology thinking before the layout thinking, and pricing the long tail honestly.
