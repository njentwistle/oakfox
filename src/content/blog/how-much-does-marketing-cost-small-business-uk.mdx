Small-business marketing budgets are usually set backwards: a number gets picked, then someone works out what to do with it. It works better the other way round. Decide what the marketing needs to achieve, what it will realistically take, and then what that costs. Here's what that looks like in the UK in 2026, with our own published prices as a reference point.

## The short answer

| Approach | Typical cost | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Do it yourself** | Your time, plus tools | Owners with time and a clear plan |
| **Freelancer for one channel** | Priced by the day, the task or a small monthly fee | Filling one specific gap, such as social or copy |
| **One-off strategy engagement** | From £3,500 at OakFox | Teams who can execute but need direction |
| **Agency retainer** | £1,500 to £6,000+ a month at OakFox | Consistent output across several channels |
| **Paid advertising** | Media spend plus management | Launches, seasonal peaks, new locations |

Most small businesses end up with a mix: a clear strategy, a couple of channels done properly, and paid media used sparingly.

## Where the money actually goes

It helps to split any marketing budget into three parts:

1. **Time.** Writing, designing, filming, posting, optimising and reporting. This is the biggest cost in organic marketing, whether you pay for it in fees or in your own evenings.
2. **Tools.** Email platform, scheduling, analytics, design software. Usually modest, and easy to overbuy.
3. **Media.** Money paid to platforms for reach: search ads, social ads, sponsorships. This only exists if you run paid campaigns, and it stops working the moment you stop paying.

Agencies mostly sell the first part. When you compare quotes, check whether a figure includes ad spend or only the fee for managing it, because the difference can be large.

## Retainer, project or in-house?

**A one-off strategy** makes sense when you have people who can do the work but no shared plan: who you're targeting, what you're saying, which channels matter, and how you'll measure it. You pay once and keep the plan.

**A retainer** makes sense when you need consistent output you can't produce yourself, month after month. The risk is paying for activity rather than results, so agree up front what gets reported.

**Doing it in-house** makes sense once marketing is central enough to justify someone's full attention. Many businesses start with a strategy engagement and grow into an in-house role later.

## What a realistic small budget can do

If money is tight, do fewer things properly:

- **Get the foundations right first.** A clear website, a complete Google Business Profile and honest, specific messaging. Marketing spend that sends people to a weak website is mostly wasted, and our [website cost guide](/blog/how-much-does-a-website-cost-uk/) covers what fixing that involves.
- **Pick one or two channels** your customers actually use, and commit to them for six months.
- **Start an email list early.** It's the one audience you own outright.
- **Write things worth finding.** Useful articles that answer your customers' real questions keep working long after they're published. They take months to build momentum, but they compound.

## How to tell if it's working

Judge marketing by the numbers that pay the bills: enquiries, bookings, sales and cost per customer. Followers, impressions and "reach" can show direction, but they aren't results. Any agency, including us, should be able to explain how its work connects to revenue, and be honest when it doesn't yet.

If sustainability is part of your marketing, make sure every claim can be backed up. UK regulators expect environmental claims to be clear, specific and evidenced. [Sustainability claims without greenwashing](/blog/sustainability-claims-without-greenwashing/) covers how to get that right.
