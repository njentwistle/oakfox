Most writing about sustainable web design quotes the same handful of global statistics. We wanted to know what the picture actually looks like for the businesses we work with — the plumbers, cafés, solicitors and salons that make up most of the UK web.

So we measured it. In July 2026 we ran the same crawler that powers our [free website carbon audit](/website-carbon-audit) over 100 real UK small-business homepages and put every one through the Sustainable Web Design Model. This is what came back.

## How we did it

We built the sample the way a customer would: searching for local businesses — "plumber Preston", "florist York", "dentist Exeter" — and taking the independent firms that appeared, skipping directories, aggregators and national chains. That gave us 100 businesses across 18 sectors and 16 towns and cities, from Exeter to Edinburgh: trades, hospitality, professional services, health, retail.

For each site we audited the homepage exactly as our public tool does. The crawler fetches the page, discovers every asset an initial visit would load — respecting lazy-loading, picking one image per srcset as a browser would, counting only the font subsets a real visitor downloads — and totals the transfer. Emissions estimates use [co2.js](https://www.thegreenwebfoundation.org/co2-js/) v4 (the Sustainable Web Design Model), and hosting is checked against The Green Web Foundation's verified directory. If anything, our method is conservative: we cap the number of assets we weigh, and we don't execute JavaScript, so anything a script loads afterwards isn't counted.

99 of the 100 sites audited successfully; one was unreachable. We're not naming individual businesses — the point is the pattern, not the finger-pointing.

## The headline numbers

| Measure | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Median homepage weight | **1.8 MB** (46 requests) |
| Mean homepage weight | 3.0 MB |
| Median emissions per view | **0.24 g CO2e** — a D rating |
| Sites rated E or F | **44%** |
| Sites rated A or A+ | 18% |
| Heaviest homepage | 22.6 MB (2.8 g CO2e per view) |
| Lightest homepage | 19 KB (0.003 g CO2e per view) |

The distribution is the story. Thirteen sites came in under half a megabyte — proof that a normal local-business site can be genuinely light. But 44% of homepages transferred more than 2 MB, 23% more than 4 MB, and eight sites more than 8 MB. The heaviest page we measured emits roughly nine hundred times more CO2e per view than the lightest, and they are both, functionally, a homepage for a local firm.

## Where the weight comes from

Across every byte we transferred in the study:

| Asset type | Share of all bytes |
| --- | --- |
| Images | **60.5%** |
| Video and audio | 15.7% |
| JavaScript | 10.2% |
| Web fonts | 9.6% |
| CSS | 2.4% |
| HTML | 1.6% |

Images dominate everything else combined. 44 of the 99 sites shipped more than 1 MB of imagery on the homepage — almost always full-resolution photographs placed straight from a camera or stock library and scaled down in the browser. The video share is concentrated in a handful of autoplaying hero videos, which are spectacular emitters: most of the 8 MB-plus club had one.

The quiet surprise was fonts. 43 sites loaded over 200 KB of web fonts — several loaded more font data than HTML, CSS and JavaScript combined. That's usually three or four weights of a display face doing a job a system font would do imperceptibly differently.

This is genuinely good news, because images are the easiest fix in web performance: modern formats (WebP/AVIF), correct dimensions, and lazy-loading below the fold routinely cut total page weight by half or more without changing how the site looks.

The pattern is intuitive: the more visual the trade, the heavier the site. Landscapers lead the table for exactly the reason you'd guess — galleries of full-resolution project photos. The two florists in our sample (too few for the table) had a median of 7.1 MB, the heaviest of any sector.

And at the bottom: garages. The humble MOT centre, with its dated-looking, no-nonsense site, is quietly running the most carbon-efficient web presence in British small business. There's a lesson in that — restraint outperforms polish, and plenty of those garage sites would also win on load time against the prettier competition.

## The green hosting mirage

On paper, the hosting picture looks encouraging: 63% of sites passed the verified green hosting check. Look closer and it softens. More than half of those passes were the site's CDN — Cloudflare or Google Cloud — being detected in front of whatever actually hosts the site. Verified green infrastructure either way, but it's an accident of using a popular CDN, not a decision anyone made.

The number we think matters more: just **26 of 99 sites** — about one in four — combined green infrastructure with an efficient page (a B rating or better). Green hosting under a 6 MB homepage is a bit like putting solar panels on a house with the windows open in January. The order of operations should be: lighten the page first, then make sure what's left is served on renewables. ## Honest scale, honest stakes

We're a sustainability-minded studio, so let's be straight about magnitude: these are small numbers per site. The median site in our sample, at a thousand visits a month, emits about 3 kg of CO2e a year from data transfer — a couple of hundred kettle boils. The heaviest would be over 30 kg. Nobody should greenwash a homepage diet into planet-saving heroism.

The stakes compound in two directions, though. There are around 5.5 million small businesses in the UK; the gap between the median site and the achievable-today lightweight version, multiplied across even a fraction of them, is real tonnage. And every one of those wasted megabytes is also wasted seconds on a phone on a patchy connection — which is where your customers actually are. The lightest sites in our study aren't ascetic; they're just built with care. That care shows up in speed, in search rankings, and in the electricity bill of the entire chain between your server and your customer's hand.
