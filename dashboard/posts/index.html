Rebranding Without Greenwashing: A Practical Checklist /blog/rebranding-without-greenwashing 27 July 2026
Category Branding Author OakFox Tags branding, sustainability, marketing, greenwashing FAQ 5 File src/content/blog/rebranding-without-greenwashing.mdx

A field-tested checklist for refreshing a brand around sustainability without overclaiming — what to verify before you say it, which claims are safe, and where businesses trip themselves up.

Open live post →

Rebrands are where overclaiming creeps in. You’ve got a new look, a renewed sense of purpose, and a designer asking what the brand stands for now — and “we’re more sustainable than we used to be” is a tempting thing to write across the top of it. The trouble is that the visual refresh moves a lot faster than the operational reality, and the gap between the two is precisely what greenwashing is.

The rules around this have tightened. The CMA’s Green Claims Code and the ASA’s enforcement of the CAP advertising rules mean a vague green claim is no longer low-risk — it’s a compliance exposure. None of that should put you off rebranding around sustainability if it’s genuine. It just means doing it carefully. Here’s the checklist we run through before a single line of green copy goes live.

Substantiate before you state

The single most important rule, and the one most rebrands get backwards. Decide what’s true first, then write the brand around it — not the other way round. The CMA’s working standard is that you must be able to substantiate a claim at the moment you make it, so every public-facing line needs something behind it: a certificate, an audit, a measured figure. If you’re writing the claim and then hoping to find the evidence later, you’ve already got it wrong. This is the same discipline we cover in detail on making sustainability claims without greenwashing.

Be specific about scope

Vague claims are the ones that fail. “Sustainable packaging” invites the question — all of it, or the box but not the liner? A claim like “our outer packaging is made from 80% recycled card” is specific, checkable, and far safer, even though it sounds less sweeping. Work through your claims and pin each one to a defined scope: which product, which material, which part of the process. The narrower and more precise the claim, the harder it is to challenge.

Avoid absolutes and comparatives you can’t back

A short list of phrases to handle with real care:

  • Absolute claims — “zero impact”, “100% sustainable”, “completely green”. These are almost impossible to evidence across a full life cycle, which is exactly the standard the Green Claims Code applies. Avoid them unless you genuinely can prove them end to end.
  • “Carbon neutral” without scope — if it rests on offsets, you have to say so, and the offsets have to be credible. Stating it bare is one of the most heavily scrutinised claims going.
  • Comparatives — “greener than before”, “more sustainable than the alternatives”. Fine in principle, but you need the baseline and the data to support the comparison, and it has to be a fair, like-for-like one.

If a claim can’t survive someone asking “compared to what, and how do you know?”, it shouldn’t be in the rebrand.

Don’t hide the trade-offs

Most real sustainability stories have a caveat, and burying it is a fast route to a complaint. If a product is recyclable but only at specialist facilities, say so. If your renewable energy covers the office but not the delivery fleet, don’t let the brand imply otherwise. The Code is explicit that claims mustn’t omit or hide material information. Being upfront about the limits of what you’ve achieved reads as more credible, not less — and it’s the honest position anyway.

Keep the evidence on file

Pull together a single folder — call it a claims register — that holds the proof for every public claim, with the dates each one applies to: energy certificates, supplier declarations, lifecycle figures, test results. Two reasons. First, if the ASA or CMA, a journalist, or a sharp customer asks how you know, you can answer quickly rather than scrambling. Second, the act of assembling it is its own audit. More than once we’ve watched a business start filling in the register and quietly drop a claim because the evidence simply wasn’t there.

Make the whole identity match the operating reality

A rebrand isn’t just a logo. It’s the copy, the photography, the packaging, the social bios, the email footer — and all of it makes claims, including the visual parts. Earthy colours, leaves, and recycled-look paper stock imply environmental credentials just as loudly as words do. The ASA has ruled against imagery that overstated a company’s green credentials even where the text was technically careful. So check the visuals against the same standard as the copy: does the whole identity reflect what the business actually does, or does it dress up an ordinary operation as a green one? If you’re commissioning this work out, build the substantiation in from the start — our notes on how to brief a branding agency cover how to fold it into the brief rather than bolting it on at the end.

Don’t imply more than you do

The subtlest failure, and the one that catches well-meaning businesses. You can be technically accurate and still mislead by implication — a single recycled product line that makes the entire company look green, or a one-off planting initiative that reads like a core commitment. Step back and ask what impression the rebrand leaves overall, not just whether each individual claim is defensible. The honest test is whether a reasonable customer would come away believing something truer than the facts. If the answer is no, scale the claims back to fit.

What safe looks like in practice is duller than the hype but a great deal sturdier. On our own site we say the work is hosted on verified renewable energy, checked through The Green Web Foundation — specific, evidenced, and easy to confirm. That’s the shape every claim in a rebrand should take.

A quick last word

A genuine sustainability story is worth telling, and a rebrand is a good moment to tell it — provided the brand never gets ahead of the business. Run your claims through this checklist before you sign anything off, and you’ll end up with an identity you can stand behind when someone asks the hard question. Managed Green Hosting vs DIY: What Small UK Businesses Actually Need /blog/managed-green-hosting-vs-diy 20 July 2026

Self-hosting on a green platform looks cheap until you count your own time. When managed green hosting earns its keep for a small UK business — and when DIY is genuinely fine.

Open live post →

There’s a version of the green hosting pitch that goes: pick a platform running on renewable energy, deploy your site, pay nothing or close to it, done. For a fair number of small UK businesses that’s genuinely the right answer, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something.

But the bill is only one column of the spreadsheet. The other column is your time, and that’s the one that quietly fills up after launch. This is the honest comparison between doing green hosting yourself and paying someone to manage it — including the cases where managed is a waste of money.

What “DIY on a green platform” actually means

The DIY route covers a few different setups, and they’re not equally demanding.

  • A static host like Cloudflare Pages or Netlify. Deploy from a git repository, sit on a CDN running on verified renewable energy, pay nothing on the free tier. Technically excellent, and we use this ourselves for plenty of sites.
  • A bare VPS on a green host. More control, more responsibility. You own the operating system, the web server, the firewall and every patch that lands on it.
  • A green shared-hosting account you administer yourself. The middle ground, where the host handles the server but you handle the site.

All three are reasonable. None of them are free in the sense that matters, because the running cost isn’t money — it’s attention.

The cost nobody puts on the invoice

When a site goes live, the work doesn’t stop. It just becomes intermittent and easy to ignore until it isn’t. Here’s the work that lands on whoever owns the site:

  • Domain and DNS. Pointing records correctly, then remembering how you did it the next time you need to change something.
  • SSL certificates. Mostly automated now, but “mostly” is doing some work in that sentence. Renewals fail, and a lapsed certificate throws a scary browser warning at every visitor.
  • Updates and patching. Dependencies, plugins, the server itself. Skip them long enough and you’ve got a security problem rather than a maintenance task.
  • Backups. Easy to set up, easy to forget, and only ever noticed at the exact moment you need one that isn’t there.
  • Uptime. If your site goes down at 2am on a Saturday, who finds out, and how long does it stay down before anyone does?

That last point is the one people underestimate. The expensive outage isn’t the dramatic one — it’s the quiet one. A form that stopped sending enquiries three weeks ago, a checkout that’s been throwing errors since the last update, a site that’s been returning a 500 to half its visitors while you got on with your actual job. You don’t pay for that in pounds. You pay for it in the customers who tried to reach you and didn’t.

Put a rough number on the time, though, because it makes the decision clearer. A typical small business site costs its owner somewhere between six and ten hours a year once you total up DNS fiddling, failed builds, update prompts and the occasional “why is the site down” panic. At a modest internal cost for that time, you’re into a few hundred pounds a year before anything has actually gone wrong.

What managed green hosting takes off your plate

Managed hosting is the deal where that list above stops being yours. The provider owns the hosting account, configures DNS, manages SSL, runs backups, monitors uptime, applies updates and patches, and gives you one person to call — ideally a UK one, in your timezone, speaking plain English rather than ticket-system boilerplate.

The “green” part means the underlying infrastructure runs on verified renewable energy. The “managed” part means you never have to touch it. For a non-technical owner, that second part is usually what they were actually paying for, even when they thought they were buying hosting.

It’s worth being straight about how this works at our end, because the model matters. OakFox is a reseller. We don’t own data centres and we don’t pretend to. We run our managed hosting on a certified green upstream host, and what we add is the layer around it: the setup, the migration, the monitoring, the support, and the fact that one accountable team handles the whole thing. We’ve written more about this trade-off in Cloudflare Pages vs managed green hosting, and if you want to see how the underlying platforms stack up, our comparison of UK green hosts lays that out without a sales angle.

The reason we can offer this at all, and the reason it’s worth your money rather than ours, is that commodity hosting is a thin business on its own. Anyone can resell a green platform. What’s actually useful is bundling the hosting with the design and development of the site itself, so there’s no finger-pointing between “the developer” and “the host” when something needs fixing. They’re the same people. That’s the bit you’re paying for.

When DIY is genuinely the right call

Managed hosting isn’t for everyone, and it’s worth saying so plainly. Self-hosting on a green platform is the better choice if:

  • You’re technical, or someone on the team is. If you’re comfortable with git, DNS and a terminal, the free tier of a static host is hard to beat on price or performance. Paying someone to manage that for you is paying to remove a problem you don’t have.
  • The site isn’t commercially critical. A side project, a personal portfolio, a marketing experiment — if a few hours of downtime costs you nothing real, the maintenance burden is low enough to absorb.
  • You enjoy it. Some owners genuinely like having their hands on the infrastructure. That’s a perfectly good reason, and the time cost doesn’t feel like a cost.

If two or three of those describe you, don’t pay for managed hosting. You’ll resent the bill and you won’t get value from the support, because you’ll never call it.

So which one do you actually need

The deciding question isn’t technical. It’s this: when the site breaks, whose problem is it, and what does that person’s hour cost?

If the answer is “mine, and my hour is better spent on the business,” managed green hosting earns its keep — not because the hosting itself is special, but because the management removes a recurring drain on your time and a category of risk you’d otherwise be carrying without noticing. If the answer is “mine, and I’m happy to fix it,” keep your money and self-host. Both are respectable. Only one of them is right for you, and it depends entirely on how you’d rather spend the hours.

If you’re not sure which camp you’re in, that’s usually a sign you’re closer to the managed end than you think — and it’s a five-minute conversation to find out. We’ll tell you honestly whether it’s worth paying us, or whether you’re better off keeping it yourself.

The problem we kept hearing about

Talk to anyone running a small ecological consultancy and you hear the same thing. The fieldwork — the part that actually needs their expertise — takes a day. The writing takes the rest of the week.

A Preliminary Ecological Appraisal site visit takes about half a day; the report takes four to five days spread across six weeks. Across all of it, experienced specialists spend forty to fifty per cent of their billable week formatting tables and retyping boilerplate that is nearly identical from one job to the next.

Biodiversity Net Gain has been mandatory in England since 2024, and the paperwork is only getting heavier. The people with the deepest field knowledge are ending up with the least time to do fieldwork. That struck us as exactly the wrong outcome for a policy meant to protect nature.

What EcoScribe does

EcoScribe takes the report writing, grant drafting, and compliance paperwork off an ecologist’s plate — drafted from the data they already collect, in their own templates, and reviewed by them before anything goes out.

  • Report automation — PEA, EIA, and BNG reports generated from field data, typically saving four to six hours per PEA.
  • Grant and scheme drafting — first-draft SFI, Countryside Stewardship, and BNG submissions from the farm and project data you already hold.
  • Compliance workflows — BNG monitoring, carbon records, and certification captured into an audit-ready archive.

The line we won’t cross

There is a wave of tools arriving in this space that promise BNG assessments “with no ecologist needed.” We think that’s the wrong idea, and we’ve built EcoScribe on the opposite principle: it never makes an ecological judgement.

Species identification, habitat evaluation, risk assessment, and survey design stay with the qualified ecologist — start to finish. EcoScribe handles the writing and administration around that judgement, not the judgement itself. It’s a tool for ecologists, built by people who think the expertise is the whole point.

Built the OakFox way

EcoScribe carries the principles OakFox is built on. Everything runs on UK-hosted, verified renewable-powered infrastructure, with model choices made with energy cost in mind. Client data is GDPR-handled, never used to train external models, and never shared between clients. What Makes a Brand Sustainable (Beyond a Green Logo) /blog/what-makes-a-brand-sustainable 6 July 2026
Category Branding Author OakFox Tags branding, sustainability, design, strategy FAQ 5 File src/content/blog/what-makes-a-brand-sustainable.mdx

Sustainable branding is a set of operating decisions, not a colour palette. What it actually means for identity, materials, messaging, and the claims you can credibly make.

Open live post →

There’s a particular kind of brand that looks sustainable and isn’t. Sage green palette, a leaf worked into the logomark, a tone of voice full of words like “conscious” and “mindful”, and a supply chain nobody has looked at too closely. The design is doing the work the operations should be doing. It’s a costume.

A genuinely sustainable brand is something quieter and harder. It’s a set of operating decisions — about materials, suppliers, longevity, and the claims you’re willing to stand behind — that happen to be expressed through design, rather than a design style pretending those decisions have been made.

Green-Looking Is Not the Same as Sustainable

Most of what gets sold as sustainable branding is aesthetic. The palette goes earthy, the photography gets some plants in it, and the copy adopts the vocabulary. None of that tells you anything about the business underneath.

This matters more than it used to. Customers have got better at spotting the costume, and regulators have started treating vague environmental language as a liability rather than a freebie. We’ve written separately about making sustainability claims without greenwashing, and the short version is that the visual identity is now the easy part. The hard part is being able to back up what the identity implies.

So it’s worth drawing a clean line. A sustainable brand is defined by two things: the operating choices the organisation actually makes, and the claims it can credibly stand behind. The design either reflects those honestly or it papers over their absence.

Longevity Is the Most Sustainable Design Decision

The single biggest lever in branding isn’t the materials — it’s how long the work lasts before it gets thrown away and redone.

A brand that chases the current visual trend dates fast. In three or four years it looks tired, the rebuild gets commissioned, and every asset — signage, packaging, print, the website — gets remade and the old stock binned. A rebuild every few years is a quiet, recurring act of waste that nobody counts because it’s spread out.

Designing for longevity is the antidote. It means choosing an identity that won’t look embarrassing when the trend turns, building a system flexible enough to absorb growth without a teardown, and resisting the decorative flourishes that age fastest. This is partly a discipline of restraint and partly a bet on the future — and it’s the same instinct we explored in the future of sustainability in branding. The identity that survives a decade has done more for the planet than any amount of recycled-paper business cards.

Restraint Reduces Real Resource Use

Restraint isn’t only an aesthetic preference. It has measurable consequences once a brand meets the physical world.

  • Print and ink. A four-colour design with heavy ink coverage and a foil finish uses more material, more energy, and more specialist stock than a considered one- or two-colour system. Full-bleed flooding of solid colour across packaging is the kind of decision that looks bold in a mockup and costs across an entire print run.
  • Energy, on the web. A brand expressed through enormous hero videos, custom fonts loaded six weights deep, and decorative animation moves more data and burns more energy every time a page loads. A lean identity renders faster and lighter — the design language and the carbon footprint pull in the same direction.
  • Specification, generally. Every spec choice — stock weight, finish, the number of components in a packaging system — is also a resource choice. Restraint usually reduces both cost and impact at once.

None of this means a sustainable brand has to look austere. It means the visual richness should come from good typography, proportion, and judgement rather than from throwing material and processing at the problem.

Accessibility Belongs in This Conversation

Accessible type and colour aren’t a separate box to tick. A brand built on thin, low-contrast type that fails on a phone in daylight quietly excludes a chunk of its audience, and tends to get patched and reworked sooner. Sufficient contrast, sensible type sizes, and a system that holds up across devices is both the inclusive choice and the durable one. Designing something a portion of your audience can’t read comfortably is its own kind of waste.

A Design System That Doesn’t Need Constant Rebuilds

The most overlooked sustainable choice is structural. A brand handed over as a folder of fixed artwork breaks the moment the business does anything new — a product line, a sub-brand, a different format — and that breakage triggers another round of design, approvals, and reprinting.

A proper design system is built to flex. Defined principles, a clear type and colour structure, components that recombine, and rules that let an in-house team extend the brand correctly without commissioning a studio every time. Most of our branding work is built this way, because a brand that can grow without a teardown is cheaper for the client and lighter on resources. The system, not the logo, is where sustainability actually lives.

Materials and Suppliers Are Brand Decisions

Branding doesn’t stop at the screen. The stock the cards are printed on, the packaging a product ships in, the printer’s energy and waste practices, the freight involved — these are all expressions of the brand, and all places where the operating choices become real.

This is where a sustainable branding agency does work an ordinary studio skips. Choosing recycled or responsibly sourced stock, specifying printers who can show their credentials, avoiding laminates and mixed materials that can’t be recycled, and helping a client think about packaging that doesn’t end up as landfill. These choices rarely show up in the portfolio shot, which is exactly why they get neglected.

Aligning the Visible Identity With Real Choices

Pull this together and the principle is simple. A sustainable brand is one where the visible identity and the operating reality match closely enough that the brand can say what it does and mean it.

When they’re aligned, the brand can make a specific, defensible claim — about a material, a supplier, a process — and stand behind it. When they’re not, you get the costume: green-looking design writing cheques the operations can’t cash, which is the precise condition that gets brands into trouble. The design’s job is to tell the truth about the business attractively, not to invent a better business than the one that exists.

For an organisation in West Lancashire or anywhere else, the honest version of this is also the more useful one. Decide what you’re genuinely willing to do — on materials, longevity, suppliers, and the claims you’ll make — and build an identity that reflects exactly that. A modest, true brand outlasts an ambitious, hollow one.

Building TrailFox: Designing an Outdoor Activity App for Families /blog/building-trailfox-outdoor-activity-app 30 June 2026
Category Product Author OakFox Tags app development, ios, android, product, ux, case study FAQ 3 File src/content/blog/building-trailfox-outdoor-activity-app.mdx

How we designed, built, and launched TrailFox — our second in-house app. The energy-and-place mechanic, designing for use outdoors in bright sunlight, shipping iOS and Android together, privacy-first handling of children's data, and why we chose one-time packs over subscriptions.

Open live post →

TrailFox is an outdoor activity app for families that we designed, built, and launched ourselves. Brand identity, UX, a cross-platform app on iOS and Android, the activity content system, store listings — every layer. It’s live now on both the App Store and Google Play.

It’s also OakFox’s second in-house product. The first, CubTrack, is a baby and toddler tracking app we shipped end-to-end and wrote about here. Building a second product is a different exercise to building a first one — you arrive with a working playbook, and the interesting decisions are the ones where you choose to break from it. This post is the inside view: what TrailFox is, the calls that shaped it, and what two in-house apps have taught the studio.

Why We Built It

The problem is one most parents will recognise. You already know the kids should be outside more. The hard part is never the principle — it’s the moment. You’ve got twenty minutes, a tired toddler, and a back garden, and your brain has gone blank. The activity ideas you find online are written for perfect weather, two free hours, and a craft cupboard nobody actually owns.

What was missing was something that meets you where you are: small, doable ideas matched to the energy in the room and the space outside the door. Not a content library you have to dig through — a suggestion that fits this moment.

There was a strategic reason too, the same one behind CubTrack. OakFox does brand, web, and development work for clients, but shipping our own products gives us somewhere to test design and technical decisions end-to-end, without compromise. Each in-house app is a working laboratory, and what proves out feeds straight back into how we approach client briefs.

The Problem in One Sentence

A parent or carer has a short window of time, a specific outdoor space, and a child with a specific amount of energy — and needs one good activity that fits all three, right now, without scrolling.

That sentence drove the whole product. Everything else is in service of it.

Brand First, Not Last

As with CubTrack, we designed the brand before writing a line of app code. Build the app first and the brand becomes a reskin that never quite fits.

TrailFox is built around curiosity and the outdoors without tipping into twee — the trap most kids’ products fall into. A fox, alert and at home in woods and gardens alike, anchors the mark, paired with a map pin and oak leaves to tie together where you are and what you find.

  • Forest (#2B5A17) — the primary colour. Grounded and natural; logo, headings, key actions.
  • Warm Cream (#F7F6F1) — the dominant background. Softer than white and, crucially, easier to read outdoors in bright light.
  • Pine (#2B564F) — a deep teal-green for activity cards and moments of focus.
  • Clay (#D4A373) — a warm terracotta for primary buttons, adding energy against the greens.
  • Amber (#FFD6A5) — a soft accent for the higher-energy options and playful highlights.

That cream background is a good example of a brand decision doing real functional work. CubTrack chose warm cream over white to ease tired eyes during night feeds. TrailFox chose it for the opposite environment — a phone held at arm’s length in direct sunlight, where pure white blows out and high-contrast cream stays legible. Same instinct, different 3am.

The Mechanic: Energy and Place

The heart of TrailFox is a deliberately tiny interaction. You tell it two things — how much energy everyone has (calm or bouncy) and where you are (garden, park, woods, street) — and it suggests an activity that fits. Don’t fancy it? Try a different one.

Getting to two inputs was the hard part. Early thinking wanted more: age, weather, time available, equipment to hand. Every one of those would have improved the match on paper and killed the product in practice. The whole value proposition is “blank brain, twenty minutes, give me something” — and a six-field form is the opposite of that. We pushed the richer context somewhere it doesn’t cost the user a decision in the moment (see Your Spot, below) and kept the front door to two taps.

Lesson for product owners: the temptation to add inputs that “improve the result” is constant, and usually wrong. Every field you add is a tax on the exact moment the product exists to rescue. Match quality and friction are in tension; pick the side your core use case lives on.

Designing for the Actual Moment

Like CubTrack, TrailFox is operated by someone whose state you have to design around — but the state is different. Outdoors, one-handed, often in bright sun, frequently with a child already pulling at the other arm.

  • Two taps to an activity from the home screen. The core loop is never more than a couple of decisions deep.
  • High-contrast type on cream so the screen survives direct sunlight, not just a design-studio monitor.
  • Short, real-life instructions — written for gardens, parks, streets, and trails as they actually are, not for ideal conditions.
  • Large, forgiving touch targets, because a phone gets tapped one-handed while the other hand holds a toddler.

These aren’t novel principles. The discipline is applying them consistently when it would be easier not to.

Shipping iOS and Android Together

Here’s the biggest break from the CubTrack playbook. CubTrack launched Android-first and brought iOS along later — a sensible call for an indie product chasing UK market share and fast iteration. TrailFox shipped both platforms at once.

Why the change? Three reasons:

  1. The codebase was built cross-platform from the first sprint. The discipline we learned keeping CubTrack’s UI cleanly separated from its business logic meant a single codebase could serve both stores without a second build effort.
  2. The audience isn’t platform-skewed. A baby tracker has a household decision-maker; an activity app for kids gets passed between two parents and a grandparent who are as likely to be on iPhone as Android. Launching one platform would have stranded half of any given family.
  3. We’d already paid the learning tax once. Apple’s Developer Program onboarding, store-listing craft, review timelines — the friction that made us launch CubTrack on Android first was a known quantity the second time.

Lesson: “launch on one platform first” is good advice for your first product and not a permanent law. The right call depends on your audience shape and how much cross-platform groundwork you’ve already laid.

Privacy by Design

TrailFox is used by adults alongside their children, so the data model was kept deliberately small. Accounts and content sync through Google Firebase, purchases run through RevenueCat and the app stores, and that is the full extent of it — no advertising, no third-party analytics, nothing sold on. Accounts and journals can be deleted in full from inside the app.

This isn’t a compliance checkbox; it’s a product position. An app aimed at families and children that quietly harvested data would be indefensible against our own no-greenwashing principles. The smallest data model that makes the product work is the right one.

One-Time Packs, Not Subscriptions

The other deliberate break from CubTrack. CubTrack runs a freemium subscription — the right model for a daily-use utility where Pro features (shared caregivers, exports, backup) earn their keep month after month.

TrailFox is used differently. It’s an occasional, joyful “what shall we do?” app, not a forty-times-a-day logger. A monthly subscription against that usage pattern is a slow leak of goodwill — you resent paying for something you open on weekends. So the model is: hundreds of activities free, and themed packs (Minibeast, Night, Mud, Beach, the four seasons, Wild Skills for older kids) as one-time purchases. No subscriptions, ever. No ads.

Lesson: don’t inherit a monetisation model from your last product. Match the pricing to how the thing is actually used. Subscription suits habitual utility; one-time purchase suits occasional delight.

Your Spot

The richer context we kept out of the front door lives here. Your Spot lets you tell TrailFox about your outdoor space once — size, water, trees, mess tolerance — so suggestions always suit where you actually are, without re-asking every time. Paired with per-child Explorer profiles, the app gets more personal the more you use it, while the moment-to-moment interaction stays at two taps. That split — heavy context stored quietly, light interaction up front — is the single design idea I’d carry into the next product.

How to verify whether your web host really runs on renewable energy — the public registry to check, what 'carbon neutral' claims actually mean, and what to do if your host fails the test.

Open live post →

Plenty of hosting companies describe themselves as green. Far fewer can show you the working. The gap between the two is wide enough that “we’re carbon neutral” has become close to meaningless as a phrase — it can mean a provider runs its data centres on wind power, or it can mean they bought some offsets last year and put a leaf on the homepage.

The good news is that you don’t have to take anyone’s word for it. There’s a public, independent way to check whether your host actually runs on renewable energy, and it takes about half a minute.

The 30-second check

The single most useful tool here is The Green Web Foundation’s public checker at thegreenwebfoundation.org/green-web-check. Type in your domain — or any domain — and it tells you whether the underlying host is in its registry of verified green providers.

The way it works is straightforward. The Foundation maintains a database of hosting companies that have submitted evidence they run on renewable energy or offset their emissions. When you check a site, it traces the IP back to whoever’s hosting it and looks them up. A green result means the host is on the registry with evidence on file. A grey result means there’s no verified evidence — which isn’t proof the host is dirty, but it does mean nobody has shown their working.

That distinction matters. “Not on the registry” and “not green” aren’t the same thing, but in practice a host that’s serious about renewable energy has every reason to be listed. If they’re not, it’s a fair question to put to them.

What ‘carbon neutral’ is actually claiming

Once you’ve run the check, the next thing worth understanding is what any green claim rests on, because not all of them are equal.

Broadly, a host can back up a renewable claim in one of a few ways, and they’re not all as good as each other:

  • Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) — long-term contracts to buy electricity from a specific renewable source, like a particular wind or solar farm. These tend to fund genuinely new clean generation, which is the outcome you actually want.
  • Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs) — tradeable certificates that let a company claim renewable energy without necessarily causing any new clean power to be built. Cheap, easy to buy in volume, and much weaker as evidence.
  • Carbon offsets — paying for emissions reductions elsewhere (tree planting, for instance) to balance out your own. This is the “carbon neutral” route, and it’s the furthest removed from running on clean power in the first place.

A host running on PPAs is doing something materially different from one that’s bought a stack of RECs or offsets and called it a day. When you’re reading a hosting company’s sustainability page, the language they use is a tell. Specifics about how they source power are a good sign. Vague mentions of being “100% carbon neutral” with no detail underneath usually mean offsets.

None of this requires you to become an energy market expert. It just means treating “carbon neutral” as the start of a question rather than the end of one.

When the check comes back grey

So your host isn’t on the registry. Now what?

Ask them directly. A short email does the job: what proportion of your energy is renewable, and how do you source it? A host that takes this seriously will answer with specifics — named agreements, percentages, maybe a link to their own reporting. A host that doesn’t will give you warm, non-committal reassurance, or nothing at all. Both responses are informative.

If the answer’s disappointing, you’ve got two routes. You can ask them to submit evidence to The Green Web Foundation, which costs them nothing and helps everyone. Or you can move. Migrating a website to a new host sounds like a faff but rarely is in practice — it’s routine work, and most decent providers will handle the move for you as part of onboarding.

It’s also worth saying that the price objection has mostly evaporated. At the entry tier, green hosting now costs roughly the same as conventional hosting. The days of paying a noticeable premium for renewable-powered servers are largely behind us, so “it’s more expensive” isn’t the obstacle it once was.

Hosting is only half the picture

Here’s the part that often gets missed. Where your site is hosted determines how clean the electricity is, but how your site is built determines how much electricity it needs in the first place. Those are two separate levers, and the second one is frequently the bigger win.

A heavy, bloated page running on renewable energy still pulls more power than a lean one — it’s just cleaner power. The most effective approach pulls on both: clean energy and a site that doesn’t waste it. A static-first build, sensible image handling and restraint with third-party scripts can cut the carbon per page view dramatically — in the region of 10 to 40 times lighter than a typical heavyweight build, depending where you’re starting from.

That’s the thinking behind our approach to sustainable web design: a fast, light site is also a low-carbon one, and the two goals reinforce each other rather than competing.

Putting it together

A green result tells you the electricity is clean. It doesn’t tell you the site is efficient, and it doesn’t tell you the underlying claim is a PPA rather than a pile of offsets.

A practical guide to writing sustainability claims that hold up to scrutiny — under UK CMA guidance, EU Green Claims Directive, and basic customer trust. With real examples of what to say, what not to say, and why.

Open live post →

Writing honest sustainability copy is harder than it sounds. The temptation is to stretch the claim — it reads better, it moves more stock, and until recently it was low-risk. That era is ending fast. UK regulators are actively investigating greenwashing, EU rules are tightening, and customers are sharper than they were five years ago. This guide covers what you can and cannot say, with real language we use on copywriting projects at OakFox.

The Regulatory Backdrop (UK and EU)

Two frameworks matter in 2026:

UK — CMA Green Claims Code

The Competition and Markets Authority’s Green Claims Code is the UK’s rulebook. It sets six principles:

  1. Claims must be truthful and accurate
  2. Claims must be clear and unambiguous
  3. Claims must not omit or hide important information
  4. Comparisons must be fair and meaningful
  5. Claims must consider the full life cycle of the product
  6. Claims must be substantiated

The CMA has been opening enforcement cases since 2022. ASOS, Boohoo, Asda, and Unilever have all been subject to investigations. Fines and undertakings are real outcomes.

EU — Green Claims Directive

The EU Green Claims Directive takes effect from 2026. If you trade in the EU, environmental claims must be:

  • Pre-verified by an accredited body before publication
  • Evidenced with specific scientific data
  • Not relying on generic offsets

“Climate neutral”, “eco-friendly”, and “green” as standalone words are on the list of prohibited vague claims.

If you trade in both markets — most UK brands do — assume the stricter EU rules.

What You Cannot Say (Without Substantiation)

Even with good intentions, certain language is now effectively prohibited or extremely risky:

AvoidWhy
”Eco-friendly”Meaningless without specificity. CMA flagged example.
”Natural”Legally ambiguous. Arsenic is natural.
”Green” as a standalone descriptorToo vague under EU Green Claims Directive.
”Carbon neutral” / “Net zero”Allowed only if the methodology is published and verified.
”Planet-positive” / “Climate-positive”Scientifically unsubstantiable at the product level.
”100% recyclable”Only if collection exists at scale in your customer’s area.
”Sustainable” without contextRequires specific, measurable backing.
”Plant-based” applied to plasticsMisleading if less than 30% bio-content.
Green/leaf/globe imagery dominating product IDCan be misleading even if copy is accurate.

What You Can Say (With Substantiation)

The good news: specific, evidenced, narrow claims are strong. Customers respond to them. Here is the pattern:

Pattern 1 — Specific input claim

Weak: “Made from sustainable cotton.”

Strong: “Woven from GOTS-certified organic cotton, grown and milled in Turkey.”

You have named the certification, the material, the process, and the geography. Everything is checkable. Customers who care can verify. Customers who do not, trust you more because the specifics signal honesty.

Pattern 2 — Specific outcome claim

Weak: “Our packaging is eco-friendly.”

Strong: “Our packaging is 100% FSC-certified cardboard with water-based inks. It is recyclable through standard UK kerbside collection.”

Two certifications cited, one input component named, and the collection route specified — so the recyclability claim does not collapse when a customer tries to recycle it.

Pattern 3 — Honest partial claim

Weak: “Sustainable supply chain.”

Strong: “Our wool comes from farms within 60 miles of the mill. The dyes are Oeko-Tex 100 certified. We do not yet know the footprint of our shipping labels — we are working on it.”

Naming the part you know about and the part you do not is the single biggest credibility move available. Customers trust brands that admit limits far more than brands that claim perfection.

Pattern 4 — Comparison claim

Weak: “Our product is better for the environment.”

Strong: “Compared to our 2022 product, this one uses 34% less virgin plastic by weight. Measured against the same SKU.”

Specify the baseline, specify the metric, specify the like-for-like condition. Claims without a baseline are unfalsifiable and therefore regulatorily risky.

Pattern 5 — Process claim

Weak: “We care about the planet.”

Strong: “We donate 1% of revenue to [named charity], independently verified in our annual report (link).”

Care is not a claim. Money and verified action is a claim.

The Language We Use at OakFox

For our own site, the sustainability language is deliberately cautious:

Deployed to verified renewable-powered hosting on The Green Web Foundation’s registry.

Not “green hosting”. Specific provider, specific verification, specific independent registry. A customer can check the claim in thirty seconds. Static-first architecture minimising energy per visit through smaller page weights and fewer requests.

Not “we care about carbon footprint”. Specific technical decision, specific mechanism, specific measurable outcome.

We do not claim ISO-accredited auditor status. For enterprise carbon accounting or CSRD reporting we refer to specialists.

Naming the limit is more credible than pretending the limit does not exist.

This is the pattern to replicate: every claim should be checkable, specific, and modest about what it does not cover.

A Simple Test

Before publishing any sustainability claim, run it through these four questions:

  1. If a regulator asked me to substantiate this in writing within 14 days, could I?
  2. If a journalist investigated this claim, is there anything in my supply chain that would embarrass me?
  3. If a customer read this claim, then read our full sourcing page, would they feel the two are consistent?
  4. If I am comparing my product to an earlier version or a competitor, is the comparison like-for-like?

If any answer is “no” or “not sure”, either gather the evidence first or rewrite the claim more narrowly.

The Commercial Case

Being careful about sustainability claims is often framed as a marketing compromise. It is the opposite. Specific claims convert better than vague ones because:

  • Trust compounds. A customer who believes your smaller claim is more likely to believe your next claim.
  • Specificity is memorable. “34% less virgin plastic” sticks; “better for the environment” does not.
  • Regulatory investigations destroy brands. The downside of a CMA case is far larger than the upside of a slightly better conversion rate.
  • Customers tell the difference. Sharp audiences increasingly recognise greenwashing and punish it with disloyalty, bad reviews, and public criticism.

The brands that have come out of the last five years strongest — Patagonia, Allbirds, Ecover, Mother of Pearl — are the ones who erred on the side of specificity. The ones quietly rebranding are the ones who did not.

Common Mistakes I See Every Month

  • Claiming something the supply chain partner has done. If your cardboard supplier is FSC certified, that does not mean your product is. The claim has to describe a change in your business, not one you inherited.
  • Using “net zero” loosely. Net zero has a specific scientific definition (SBTi methodology). Most “net zero” claims would not survive a technical review.
  • Mixing aspirations with achievements in the same sentence. “We use organic cotton and are working toward biodegradable packaging” — the reader assumes both are current.
  • Relying on offsets. Carbon offsets have fallen significantly in regulatory favour. A sustainability strategy that leans on offsets is a strategy with a short shelf life.
  • Putting sustainability on the homepage when it is not in the product. If the first thing a customer reads is sustainability copy, the product better deliver. If it does not, the homepage becomes the weakness, not the strength.

If In Doubt

If in doubt about a claim, do one of three things:

  1. Rewrite narrower. Name the specific mechanism and omit the broader claim.
  2. Gather evidence first. Ship the data, then ship the copy.
  3. Drop it. Not every page needs a sustainability line. A product page that does not claim anything is more trustworthy than one that overclaims.

Most articles on “sustainable web design” are marketing dressed up as advice. This one tries not to be. If you run or commission websites in the UK and want to know what actually reduces their environmental impact — without the platitudes — this is the practical version.

What Sustainable Web Design Actually Means

The internet is responsible for roughly 3.7% of global carbon emissions — comparable to the aviation industry. Every page view draws energy from data centres, network infrastructure, and the device in the user’s hand. Sustainable web design reduces that draw without compromising the experience.

It is not the same as “eco-friendly design” — there is no moss on your homepage, no green leaf logo. It is an engineering discipline:

  • Less data transferred per visit (smaller images, leaner code, fewer requests)
  • Less compute required to render (static HTML where possible, minimal JavaScript)
  • Infrastructure running on renewable energy (verified green hosting)
  • Longevity (sites that do not need rebuilding in two years when the framework falls out of fashion)

Get those four right and you have done 90% of the work.

Measure Before You Change Anything

Before cutting kilobytes, find out where you actually stand. The tools:

  • OakFox Website Carbon Audit — our free checker: CO2 per visit, page weight, and a green hosting check in one pass, with an A+ to F grade. No signup.
  • Website Carbon Calculator — estimates grams of CO2 per page view. A good starting benchmark.
  • Ecograder — scores pages on green design principles and lists specific issues.
  • Google PageSpeed Insights — measures Core Web Vitals, which correlate strongly with energy efficiency.
  • The Green Web Foundation — checks whether your host runs on renewable power.

A typical UK marketing site we inherit comes in at 2–4g CO2 per page view on a grid-powered host. Sites we rebuild land between 0.1g and 0.3g on a green host — a 10× to 40× improvement. That is the magnitude of the opportunity.

The Highest-Leverage Changes

Not all changes move the needle equally. In order of impact for a typical UK marketing site:

1. Switch to a verified green host

This is the single highest-leverage change. Moving from a generic grid-powered host to one on The Green Web Foundation’s verified list can cut emissions per visit by 60–80% with no code change at all. We maintain a working comparison of the green web hosting UK providers we actually use, with current prices and honest caveats. Reliable options used by OakFox-built sites:

  • Cloudflare Pages — verified green, free tier, excellent performance
  • Netlify — 100% renewable energy for infrastructure
  • Vercel — runs on matched renewable power
  • Krystal (UK-based) — verified green hosting with UK data centres

Cost is usually neutral or lower than legacy hosting. There is no reason not to move. If you would rather not manage hosting yourself, our managed green web hosting service handles the migration, setup, and ongoing maintenance on verified renewable-powered UK infrastructure.

2. Optimise images properly

Images are almost always the largest portion of page weight. The wins:

  • Use modern formats — AVIF where supported, WebP as fallback, JPEG last. AVIF is typically 50% smaller than JPEG at the same perceptual quality.
  • Size images to purpose — stop serving 4000px-wide hero images that will be displayed at 1200px.
  • Use responsive srcset — serve smaller images to smaller screens.
  • Lazy-load below the fold — native loading="lazy" on <img> is free.
  • Drop unnecessary images — ask “does this image add information, or is it decoration?” Decoration is often a waste.

A typical marketing site with eight to fifteen images can cut its total weight by 60% without changing a single pixel of final displayed resolution.

3. Reduce JavaScript

JavaScript is the most energy-expensive part of a typical website. Every kilobyte shipped takes bandwidth to download, CPU to parse, memory to hold, and battery to execute — multiplied across every visitor’s device.

Practical cuts:

  • Static rendering — use Astro, 11ty, Hugo, or similar to pre-render HTML. Most marketing pages do not need SPA frameworks.
  • Remove unused libraries — audit the site with Chrome DevTools “Coverage” panel. Libraries unused on most pages are candidates for deletion.
  • Defer what is not critical — analytics, chat widgets, and embedded third-party code should load after the main content renders, not before.
  • No tag managers if they are not earning their weight — GTM alone ships 50–80KB before it loads anything useful.

4. Clean, semantic HTML and restrained CSS

Less code is less to download, parse, and render. Tailwind CSS (which we use extensively) is deceptive on this axis — the raw source is large but the compiled production CSS typically comes out at 5–15KB gzipped because unused classes are purged. Verify the production bundle.

5. Efficient fonts

Web fonts are usually the second-largest source of page weight after images. Cuts:

  • Subset to the characters you actually need — Latin Extended is rarely necessary for an English-language site
  • Use font-display: swap — shows system font first, swaps to custom font when loaded
  • Preconnect to your font host — cuts DNS and TLS overhead
  • Consider variable fonts — a single variable font file often replaces three or four static weight files

Accessibility Is Part of Sustainability

Accessible websites are faster, leaner, more durable, and work for more people. WCAG 2.2 AA is not a separate discipline from sustainable web design — it is the same discipline.

Specifics that compound both accessibility and energy efficiency:

  • Semantic HTML — <button>, <nav>, <main>, <article>. Screen readers use them. So do search engines. And they replace JavaScript-heavy custom components.
  • Keyboard navigation from the start — every interactive element reachable and operable without a mouse.
  • Colour contrast ratios of 4.5:1 (text) and 3:1 (UI) — verified with tools, not eyeballed.
  • Alt text written with intent — not a machine-generated placeholder.
  • Focus states visible — never outline: none without a replacement.

We target WCAG 2.2 AA as a minimum on every OakFox project. Accessibility overlays (AccessiBe, UserWay) are not a substitute — they ship more JavaScript, frequently break sites, and have been the subject of growing litigation.

Design for Longevity

The most sustainable website is the one you do not have to rebuild. Choices that extend useful life:

  • Avoid frameworks of the month — the framework decision sets your maintenance floor for the next five years. Pick stable, well-funded options.
  • Design systems over one-off pages — tokens, components, and patterns extend a site’s useful life far longer than bespoke designs.
  • Separate content from structure — content in a CMS or Markdown files, not hardcoded in layouts. Lets you refresh copy without touching code.
  • Write documentation assuming you will not be around — future maintainers need to understand what you built and why.

Every rebuild is a carbon cost. Every year you extend a site’s useful life reduces that cost.

What Sustainable Web Design Is Not

A few things sustainable web design is not, because the term gets abused:

  • It is not dark mode alone. Dark mode saves meaningful energy on OLED screens but has zero effect on LCDs or the server side. Useful, but marginal.
  • It is not a Wix site with a green leaf in the logo. Low-code platforms often ship enormous page weights and lock you into infrastructure with no green credentials.
  • It is not a carbon offset. Offsets are a credibility hazard, not a solution. Reduce first, offset only the residue, and be transparent about both.
  • It is not a one-off audit. Sites gain weight over time. Sustainability is a maintenance discipline, not a launch achievement.

Practical Next Steps

If you have an existing website and want to make it more sustainable:

  1. Benchmark it — run Website Carbon Calculator, Ecograder, and PageSpeed on your top five pages.
  2. Move to a green host — biggest single lift, usually one weekend of work.
  3. Audit images — formats, sizing, lazy-loading. A good afternoon’s work for most small sites.
  4. Remove unused JavaScript — kill the tag manager if it is not earning its keep.
  5. Add WCAG 2.2 AA as a standing requirement — every new feature, every review.

If you are commissioning a new site, the brief should include:

  • Verified green hosting as a deliverable
  • A performance budget (page weight, Largest Contentful Paint target)
  • WCAG 2.2 AA conformance as a non-negotiable
  • A design system handed over as part of the project
  • Documentation written for maintenance, not marketing

How to choose a brand name that works commercially and legally. The process we use at OakFox — with a checklist, worked examples, and the mistakes to avoid.

Open live post →

Naming is the single hardest decision in a new brand. It is the one thing almost impossible to change cleanly once momentum builds, and the one thing everyone in the room has an opinion on. This is the process we use at OakFox — refined over 15 years of brand identity projects — to turn naming from a fraught democratic exercise into a structured decision.

The Process at a Glance

If you want the shape of it before the detail, this is the six-stage process — roughly a day and a half of work, then a decision:

  1. Define the constraints (1 hour) — audience, positioning, tone, an avoid-list.
  2. Generate a longlist (4–6 hours) — 80–120 candidates, no judging yet.
  3. Cull to a shortlist (2–3 hours) — down to 15–20.
  4. Legal & commercial checks (half a day) — trademark, domain, social, international meaning.
  5. Stress-test the survivors (1 hour each) — the radio test, the ten-year test.
  6. Choose and commit — within a week, no longer.

What a Good Brand Name Has to Do

A brand name is not a poem. It is a working asset that has to:

  1. Be memorable — pronounceable, spellable, and reducible to a single clear idea.
  2. Be legally available — trademarkable in your jurisdiction, with a useable .com or relevant domain.
  3. Survive internationalisation — not mean something unfortunate in a language you expect customers to speak.
  4. Fit the positioning — a luxury wine merchant needs a different name from a low-cost coffee chain.
  5. Age well — not be so on-trend that it dates in three years.
  6. Be flexible — leave room for the brand to evolve without rebrand paralysis.

The best names rarely excel at all six. Most nail three or four and hold their own on the rest.

The Process

Stage 1 — Define the constraints (1 hour)

Before generating a single candidate, write down:

  • What the brand is and does — one paragraph, same as you would put in a branding brief.
  • Who it is for — primary audience, language, cultural context.
  • Positioning — premium, accessible, disruptive, heritage, etc.
  • Tonal boundaries — should it be warm or cold? Serious or playful? Traditional or modern?
  • Phonetic preferences — hard consonants (commanding, memorable: Nike, Kodak) or soft consonants (warmer, more approachable: Aesop, Warby Parker)? Short (2 syllables, punchy) or longer (more substantive)?
  • Avoid list — sectors, competitors, archetypes you do not want to echo.

Writing this down forces clarity and gives you a filter to judge candidates against. If you skip it, you will debate naming for weeks in circles.

Stage 2 — Generate a longlist (4–6 hours)

Aim for 80–120 candidate names. You need volume to find the good ones. Use multiple naming strategies in parallel. The six that matter, and the trade-off each carries:

StrategyWhat it isTrademark strengthMarketing costExample
DescriptiveSays what the business doesWeakLowThe Body Shop
EvocativeSuggests an idea or feelingStrongMediumPatagonia, Tesla
InventedA made-up wordStrongestHighestKodak, Google
Founder-basedA person’s nameMediumLow–mediumJo Malone
CompoundTwo words fusedGoodMediumFacebook, OakFox
MetaphoricalBorrowed from nature or mythStrongMediumNike, Apple

In detail:

  • Descriptive — directly describes what the business does (BookingCom, General Motors, The Body Shop). Easy to understand, hard to trademark, limited flexibility.
  • Evocative — suggests an idea or feeling without being literal (Patagonia, Tesla, Amazon). Stronger trademarks, more memorable, need more marketing to explain.
  • Invented — made-up words (Kodak, Xerox, Google). Best trademarks, highest marketing cost, biggest upside.
  • Founder-based — uses a person’s name (Jo Malone, Aesop Rock, Warren James). Works for certain sectors, harder to sell the business later.
  • Compound — two existing words fused (Facebook, SnapChat, OakFox). Middle ground — memorable, reasonably trademarkable.
  • Metaphorical — borrowed from nature, mythology, or culture (Nike after the Greek goddess, Apple for simplicity). Works well when the metaphor is apt.

Do not judge at this stage. Get to 80. A good exercise: ban editing for the first hour and just write.

Stage 3 — Cull to a shortlist (2–3 hours)

Now judge. Against your Stage 1 constraints, remove anything that:

  • Is actively bad when read aloud three times
  • Requires explanation every time it is said
  • Echoes a competitor too closely
  • Has a cringe-inducing connotation
  • Is obviously hard to spell or pronounce

You want to land on 15–20 candidates.

Stage 4 — Legal and commercial checks (half a day)

For each shortlisted candidate:

  • UK IPO trademark search — ipo.gov.uk/tmcase. Free. Check classes relevant to your business.
  • EUIPO search if you expect to trade in the EU — euipo.europa.eu/eSearch/.
  • Companies House search — gov.uk/get-information-about-a-company. Not a deal-breaker if someone shares a name in an unrelated sector, but worth knowing.
  • Google the name — existing businesses, news stories, anything awkward on the first page.
  • Domain availability — .com ideal, .co.uk acceptable for UK-first brands, .shop / .studio / .design niche.
  • Social handle availability — check Instagram, LinkedIn, and the platforms your sector uses. Use namecheckr.com as a quick starter.
  • International meaning check — drop candidates into Google Translate across five or six languages. An uncomfortable number of startup names mean something inappropriate in German, Portuguese, or Swedish.

This stage typically kills half your shortlist. That is the point.

Stage 5 — Stress test the survivors (1 hour per candidate)

For the 5–7 candidates that pass legal and commercial checks, stress-test each:

  • The radio test — can a friend understand it over a bad phone line and spell it correctly?
  • The ten-year test — will this name still feel right in 2036?
  • The positioning test — if you described your brand as the name out of context, what would a stranger guess the business sells? Is it close to what you actually do?
  • The logo test — sketch a logotype in three different weights. Does it hold together visually? Short punchy names win here; long ones struggle.
  • The “what’s it called?” test — after a customer tells a friend about you, what will they remember of the name? First syllable? Shape of the word? Misremembered variant?

Stage 6 — Choose, and commit

The final step is the hardest: pick one and commit. Do not let the decision drag past a week. Ambiguity compounds — every day you do not commit is a day you cannot build the website, register the company, order signage, or brief a designer.

If two candidates are genuinely tied, pick the one that is shorter, easier to trademark, and less likely to be confused with a competitor. That is almost always the right call.

Worked Examples

When we named Aigéan, the Irish seafood restaurant, the client wanted something that communicated Irish provenance without being a cliché. “Aigéan” is the Irish word for “ocean” — pronounced roughly “AG-awn”. Chosen because:

  • Direct connection to the business (seafood, ocean)
  • Distinctive in a Dublin market full of English-language names
  • Trademarkable as a non-obvious word in food services
  • Short, two syllables, holds together in a logotype
  • Unpronounceable on first sight but becomes a gentle education for diners — part of the brand story

Tradeoffs accepted: customers will mispronounce it the first time. The team now treat every pronunciation correction as an opening to talk about the menu.

Roll

For Roll, the Lake District bakery and coffee shop, we went the opposite direction:

  • A single-syllable word
  • Directly evocative of the product (bread rolls, coffee rolls, roll of ground coffee)
  • Simple logotype potential
  • Trademarkable in hospitality with specific application
  • Internationally universal — the word reads similarly across English-speaking markets

Tradeoffs accepted: “Roll” on its own is undifferentiated in search. We specified “Roll Bakery” in the extended word mark and anchored search listings to the town name (“Roll, Bowness”) for local discovery.

CubTrack

For CubTrack, the baby and toddler app, the name had to do immediate functional work:

  • “Cub” — universally understood reference to young mammals, warm connotation
  • “Track” — direct signal of the app’s function (tracking feeding, sleep, development)
  • Compound word — strong trademark potential in the app store category
  • Short, easy to search, obvious domain fit
  • Works across iOS and Android store listings at first glance

Tradeoffs accepted: some parents find “cub” overly cute. We addressed this in the brand voice — spare, helpful, practical — so the product feel balances the name.

Common Mistakes

  • Naming by committee. Five people will never agree on a single name. Two people who trust each other can.
  • Falling in love with the first idea you liked. The first name that feels right is usually the one you are emotionally attached to, not the best candidate. Kill it deliberately and see what survives.
  • Picking a name you cannot buy the .com for. Exceptions exist (Warby Parker is warbyparker.com, Canva is canva.com — both exact match) but in 2026 the cost of failing to secure a strong domain compounds. If the exact .com is taken by a parked page, it is almost always worth paying the four-figure asking price for a viable brand.
  • Trademarking after launching. Launch with a name, spend six months building equity, then discover the trademark is unavailable — it happens, and the rebrand costs far more than the £170 UK IPO application you should have filed at the start.
  • Ignoring pronunciation. “Aigéan” is intentional ambiguity. A badly-chosen unpronounceable name is just a barrier to recommendation.
  • Choosing a name that boxes you in. A name that is too literal constrains future pivots. If “Corner Coffee” decides to sell tea, the brand is broken.

Since people ask: OakFox is a compound — oak for stability, longevity, slow growth; fox for adaptability, cunning, local knowledge. We picked it because the work we do sits at the intersection of those two ideas. Sustainable branding is the same bet — things that last because they are adaptable, rather than rigid.

A practical guide to writing a brief that gets you a better proposal, a better quote, and a better project. With the questions to answer, the mistakes to avoid, and a template you can actually use.

Open live post →

Bad briefs produce bad proposals. Agencies guess, quotes balloon, scope creeps, and by the time the project starts nobody is clear what they are actually building. The fix is simple and free — spend an hour writing a brief that gives the agency enough to reply properly.

This is how we write them, what we look for in the ones we receive, and what to skip.

What a Brief Is Actually For

A branding brief is not a specification document. You are not telling the agency what to make. You are telling them who you are, what you need to achieve, and what the constraints are — so they can come back with a considered proposal and an accurate quote.

A good brief gets you:

  • A more accurate fixed-price quote (fewer unknowns = fewer contingency buffers)
  • Agencies that are genuinely suited to the work self-selecting in, and the rest self-selecting out
  • A faster kick-off — the agency has already done 60% of the discovery before day one
  • A reference document you can both return to when scope disputes come up

The Eight Things Every Branding Brief Should Cover

1. Who you are, in one paragraph

Not your “about us” page. A plain-language description of the business.

Aigéan is a seafood restaurant in Sandymount, Dublin, launching in autumn 2026 by chef [X]. It will serve Irish-caught, Michelin-quality seafood at fair prices, sourced from named boats and landed within 24 hours. The dining room seats 40.

That is enough. You are orienting the reader.

2. Why you are doing this now

What has changed? A launch? A merger? A reposition? Coming up to the ten-year mark and the brand no longer fits? Regulatory pressure? A new market?

The agency’s approach should change significantly depending on why. A launch brand is greenfield. A reposition is political — there are internal stakeholders, legacy decisions, and things that are emotionally hard to change. Different skill.

3. Who the brand is for

Be specific. “Everyone” or “professionals” is not an audience. “32–55 year old parents in urban West Lancashire who care about where their food comes from and have a household income above £60k” is an audience.

If you have existing customer research — interviews, segmentation data, NPS responses — include summaries. Even informal observations (“our best customers tend to be X”) are useful.

4. What success looks like

Not a number necessarily — though numbers help. The outcome you are actually trying to move:

  • Higher average transaction value?
  • Better margin on the same revenue?
  • Justifying a 30% price increase?
  • Making the business investable / saleable?
  • Being taken seriously by retailers you have been rejected by?
  • Attracting a different kind of customer?

Be honest about this. A rebrand to “make us look more modern” is a vanity exercise; a rebrand to “justify a price position that puts us alongside Daylesford rather than Waitrose” is a commercial one. Different amounts of work.

5. The competitive landscape

Three to six competitors or adjacent brands, with a short note on each about what works and what does not. Not exhaustive — a sample.

Also include brands you admire from outside your sector. This is more useful than it sounds. If a restaurant says they admire the visual restraint of Aesop, the graphic discipline of The Financial Times, and the tone of Ben’s Cookies, you have already narrowed the design direction enormously.

6. The scope you think you need

Do not write a specification — write your best guess. The agency will adjust.

We think we need:

  • A new wordmark and logo system
  • A visual identity (colour, typography, photographic direction)
  • Guidelines our team and future collaborators can use
  • Initial application: menus, signage, website, social templates

If you do not know, say “we do not know — please recommend.” That is a legitimate brief.

7. Budget and timeline

The single most common brief mistake: refusing to share budget. The agency cannot tell you what is possible without knowing what you can spend. Sharing a budget range — even a rough one — filters in agencies that work at your scale and filters out the ones that do not.

If your budget is £8k–£15k, say so. Agencies that cannot do quality work at that level will pass politely. The ones that can will propose accordingly.

For timelines, share:

  • When you ideally want to launch the new brand — with any fixed dates (events, funding, product launches, property leases)
  • Which dates are immovable and which are flexible
  • Who the key decision-makers are, and when they are available

8. Who decides

List the people who will approve work, by name and role. How many rounds of reviews do you typically need? Are there stakeholders who need sign-off but are not hands-on (an investor, a co-founder, a spouse who always gets a vote)?

This is not a bureaucratic question. A three-person studio with one clear decision-maker can run a project in half the time of a six-person team where every review involves five people with veto power. Agencies price accordingly — and should.

What to Leave Out

  • Preferred design directions (“we want minimalist”). You are hiring the agency because they are better at this than you. If you have dictated the answer before they start, you have wasted the brief.
  • Long mood boards. One or two reference brands is useful context. Twenty reference brands is a sign you have not decided what you want.
  • Specifications of colours or typefaces. The agency will propose these.
  • Excessive constraints on methodology. “We want to start with a workshop” is a scope instruction, not a brief.
  • Detailed organisational history. One paragraph max. The agency will read your website.

The Common Mistakes

  • Writing the brief after you have already picked a supplier. You will get a smooth process but a worse outcome than a proper competitive brief to three studios.
  • Sharing only with agencies you found via Google. The best agencies for specific work are often recommended, not discovered. Ask founders in your sector who they used.
  • Asking for “free pitches” of actual design work. No reputable agency will do speculative design work. What you should ask for is a proposal: their read on the brief, their team, their process, their price, their timeline.
  • Not giving a response deadline. A brief with no deadline is a brief that will get a reply whenever. Set one. Two to three weeks is reasonable for a proposal.
  • Forgetting to say what happens next. Will you shortlist three? Interview two? What is the decision process? Agencies will spend more effort on the proposal if they know how it will be evaluated.

The Template

Copy, adapt, send:

Branding Brief: [Business Name]

1. About us (one paragraph)
2. Why we are doing this now
3. Who our customers are (and are not)
4. What success looks like in 12 months
5. Three to six competitors, with a note on each
6. Brands we admire (in or out of sector)
7. What we think we need (scope guess)
8. Budget range — £[low]–£[high]
9. Timeline — ideal launch date, any fixed dates
10. Decision makers and approval process
11. Deadline for your proposal
12. How we will decide

[Contact details]

An hour with this template beats a month of misaligned back-and-forth.

One Final Thing

If the agency comes back asking clarifying questions before they quote, that is a good sign. It means they read the brief, noticed what was missing, and want to quote accurately. The agencies that come back instantly with a high-confidence number and no questions are usually the ones you should not hire.
