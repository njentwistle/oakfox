What a Preliminary Ecological Appraisal Actually Contains — and Why Most Take Six Weeks Longer Than They Should

OakFox · 27 April 2026
ecology pea preliminary-ecological-appraisal consultancies planning

If you have ever commissioned a Preliminary Ecological Appraisal — the standard first ecology report on almost any UK planning application — you will know the rhythm. You ask for it in March. The site visit happens within a fortnight. Then it goes quiet for six weeks. Eventually a forty-page PDF arrives, the planners ask one question, and a revised version comes back ten days later.

The fieldwork on a typical PEA takes half a day. The writing takes the rest of the project. That ratio is the entire story of why these reports cost what they do.

This post is a plain walk-through of what is actually in a PEA, what part of it genuinely needs an experienced ecologist, and why so much of the elapsed time is spent on tasks that — honestly — should not be the bottleneck.

What a PEA Is For

A Preliminary Ecological Appraisal sits at the front end of the planning process. It is meant to answer two questions for a Local Planning Authority. First: what habitats and protected species could reasonably be present on this site? Second: what further survey work, mitigation, or design changes does the project need to satisfy planning policy and the law?

It is following the CIEEM Guidelines for Preliminary Ecological Appraisal — the industry-standard methodology — and it is almost always the document that determines whether a planning application sails through or stalls for a season.

The brief is straightforward. The execution is not.

What’s Actually in the Document

A standard PEA, regardless of who writes it, contains the same broad sections. The proportions shift with the site, but the structure is near-identical job to job.

Site context and proposal description. A short summary of the location, the existing land use, and what is being proposed. Usually pulled from planning documents, an OS map, and a developer brief.

Desk study. A review of statutory and non-statutory designated sites within a defined search radius — typically 2 km for local sites, 10 km for European sites, with bat-relevant searches sometimes wider. Records from the local Biological Records Centre, MAGIC, the relevant Wildlife Trust, and historical aerial imagery. This is the bit that produces the long table of designations and the inevitable paragraph about the SSSI four miles away.

Habitat survey results. A UK Hab or Phase 1 walkover, with target notes, a habitat plan, and descriptions of each habitat parcel. This is the field component, and it’s the section where genuine ecological judgement does the work.

Protected species assessment. For each relevant species group — bats, great crested newts, badgers, reptiles, breeding birds, hedgehogs, dormice, water voles, otters, and any locally relevant species — an evaluation of likelihood of presence and a recommendation on whether further survey is required. Most of this is structured prose: “Suitable habitat is present in the form of X. Further survey is recommended in the form of Y, between dates Z.”

Invasive species check. A quick note on any Schedule 9 species observed.

Constraints and recommendations. The bit the planners actually read first. A summary of what surveys are needed, what mitigation should be designed in, and what the consultant recommends as next steps.

Appendices. Photo plates, the habitat plan, the desk study results table, sometimes a target note schedule.

A reasonable PEA for a small to medium site lands somewhere between thirty-five and seventy pages.

Where the Time Actually Goes

This is where the gap between perception and reality opens up.

A small ecological consultancy quoting on a PEA is typically pricing in something like a half-day site visit, half a day of desk study, two to three days of report writing, and a half-day of QA and admin. Four to five working days, spread across three to six weeks of elapsed time because the ecologist is juggling six other projects in survey season.

Of those four to five days, only the half-day of fieldwork and parts of the protected species assessment require senior ecological judgement. Everything else is structured, repetitive, and — critically — almost identical job to job.

The desk study is a mechanical exercise. The same data sources, the same search radii, the same boilerplate around how the data was obtained and what its limitations are. The habitat descriptions follow standard CIEEM-aligned language. The protected species sections are largely a structured walk-through of a checklist where each species group has a near-fixed paragraph that gets adjusted based on what was observed on site. The constraints summary pulls from the species assessments. The appendices are formatting.

A senior ecologist writing all of this from scratch on a Tuesday afternoon is, in effect, a £350-a-day specialist doing £80-a-day work. And then doing the same work, in slightly different words, for the next site three weeks later.

Why This Matters

The bottleneck is not skill. It is throughput.

Small consultancies — the ones doing the most careful site work — are the most exposed to this. A two-person practice cannot afford to dedicate someone to standardising templates, building structured input forms, or systemising the desk study. They are too busy doing the next job. So every PEA gets typed out from a slightly-newer version of the last one, with the same language being polished from scratch every time.

The downstream cost is real. Clients wait six weeks for what should — based on the actual cognitive load — be a two-week deliverable. Survey-season windows close while reports sit in queues. Planning timelines slip. The ecologist with the field experience worth paying for spends most of their week formatting tables.

This is the same pattern we wrote about with BNG — the writing around the work has quietly become more burdensome than the work itself.

What a Faster PEA Looks Like

It is worth saying clearly: a faster PEA is not a worse PEA. It is the same report, with the parts that don’t need a senior ecologist not done by a senior ecologist.

The shape of the fix has three parts.

Structured input at the point of fieldwork. Habitat parcels, target notes, photos, GPS coordinates, and species observations captured on a phone or tablet on site, in the format the report needs them. Not as scribbled field notes that get retyped two days later.

A single source of truth for desk study. The 2 km and 10 km searches, the MAGIC layers, the local records, the aerial history — pulled once, structured properly, and reused across the document rather than rephrased in each section.

Templated narrative that adapts to the survey data. Standard CIEEM-aligned language for each habitat type and each protected species group, parameterised by what was actually observed. The senior ecologist reviews and refines the output. They do not type the boilerplate from scratch.

That is roughly the difference between a four-day report and a one-day report. Same content, same standards, same field judgement. Less retyping.

The Bit That Matters

None of this is about replacing ecologists. The judgement of whether a feature is suitable for roosting bats, whether a watercourse is reasonably likely to support water vole, whether an apparent badger sett is active — that is the work, and it stays with the ecologist.

But the structured production of a forty-page document around that judgement is no longer where senior consultancy time is well spent. The practices that figure this out first will be the ones still taking on PEAs in five years. The ones that don’t will quietly cap their capacity at whatever their current admin overhead allows.

We’re building tools in this space and would be glad to hear from anyone running a small consultancy who recognises this. It’s the sort of thing we like thinking about.
