Carbon Neutral vs Green Web Hosting: The Difference, and Which to Pick

OakFox · 25 August 2026
green hosting sustainability web development uk

Two labels appear on UK hosting websites, often side by side and rarely explained: green and carbon neutral. They sound like synonyms. They are not — they describe two different mechanisms, they support two different claims, and if you care which one you’re buying, it’s worth two minutes to understand the gap.

What green hosting means

Green hosting, used carefully, means the servers run on renewable electricity. The provider either buys renewable power directly, holds renewable energy certificates that match its consumption, or runs in data centres that do. The emissions are avoided at the point of use — the electricity powering your website wasn’t generated by burning anything.

The claim is checkable. The Green Web Foundation maintains a directory of hosting providers that have submitted evidence — certificates, power purchase agreements — and verifies them. Any site can be checked against it in seconds, which is exactly what our carbon audit tool does as part of its hosting check. We’ve written before about what’s actually behind a green hosting badge, because the verification matters more than the logo.

What carbon neutral means

Carbon neutral is an accounting claim, not an energy claim. The provider measures its emissions — which may come from entirely conventional electricity — and then compensates for them, almost always by buying carbon offsets: funding tree planting, renewable projects, or capture schemes that notionally cancel out what was emitted.

Done rigorously, that’s a legitimate position, and meaningfully better than doing nothing. But notice what the claim doesn’t say: it doesn’t tell you the energy was clean, and it makes the whole promise only as strong as the offsets behind it — their quality, permanence, and whether they’d have happened anyway. That’s difficult for a buyer to evaluate, which is why UK advertising guidance under the CMA’s Green Claims Code expects offset-based claims to be disclosed as offset-based rather than left to imply clean energy. The same discipline we recommend for any sustainability claim a business makes applies to the hosting industry’s claims about itself.

The difference in one sentence

Green hosting tries not to emit in the first place; carbon neutral hosting emits, then compensates. Prevention versus apology — and while a sincere apology has value, prevention is the stronger position and the easier one to verify.

Which should you pick?

Our order of preference, for what it’s worth as a studio that hosts client sites:

  1. Verified renewable first. A host in The Green Web Foundation’s directory has shown evidence to a third party. That’s the claim you can repeat on your own website without wincing — specific, checkable, dated.
  2. Carbon neutral as a supplement, not a substitute. A renewable-powered host that also offsets its residual emissions (buildings, travel, hardware) is doing it properly. A fossil-powered host that only offsets is making the weakest version of the claim.
  3. Either beats neither — but only if it’s verifiable. An unverifiable green claim on your hosting flows straight through to an unverifiable green claim on your website, and that’s now a compliance question, not just a taste one.

And remember the order of operations from our July study of 100 UK small-business sites: hosting is the second fix. A heavy page on green infrastructure still emits more than a light page on it. Lighten the site, then get the hosting right underneath — the two together are what a genuinely low-carbon website looks like.

Checking any host in sixty seconds

  • Search the provider, or your own domain, in The Green Web Foundation’s directory — or run your site through our free audit, which includes the check automatically.
  • If they’re not listed, email and ask what stands behind the claim: certificates, power purchase agreements, or offsets — and if offsets, which scheme. A credible host answers in a sentence.
  • If the answer is vague, you’ve learned something too.

Our own answer, for the record: OakFox’s managed green hosting runs on infrastructure verified through The Green Web Foundation, from £9 a month with migration included — and the claim is worded exactly as narrowly as the evidence supports, which is rather the point of this post.

Frequently asked

Common questions

Is carbon neutral hosting the same as green hosting?
No. Green hosting generally means the infrastructure runs on renewable energy, so the emissions are low in the first place. Carbon neutral hosting means the provider has measured its emissions — which may come from any energy source — and compensated for them, usually by buying offsets. A host can be carbon neutral without using any renewable energy at all, which is why the two labels aren't interchangeable.
Is offsetting-based hosting greenwashing?
Not automatically — measurement plus honest compensation is far better than nothing, and some providers do it rigorously. But the claim depends entirely on the quality of the offsets and the transparency of the accounting, which is hard for a customer to verify. UK guidance under the CMA's Green Claims Code expects offset-based claims to be disclosed as such. Renewable-powered hosting is simply an easier claim to check.
How do I verify a green hosting claim?
Check the host — or the site running on it — against The Green Web Foundation's directory, which verifies providers' renewable energy evidence and is free to search. If a provider isn't listed, ask them directly what stands behind the claim: renewable energy certificates, a power purchase agreement, or offsets. A credible host answers quickly; a vague answer is itself an answer.
Does green web hosting cost more?
Not meaningfully at the small-business end. Verified renewable-powered hosting is available in the UK at ordinary shared-hosting prices — our own managed plans start at £9 a month, migration included. For a typical small-business site the price difference between green and fossil-powered hosting has essentially disappeared; the difference that remains is whether anyone verified the claim.
