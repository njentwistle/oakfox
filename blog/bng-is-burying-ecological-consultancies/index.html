Why BNG is Burying Small Ecological Consultancies in Paperwork

OakFox · 14 April 2026
ecology bng regulation consultancies sustainability

Biodiversity Net Gain has been mandatory for major developments in England since February 2024, and for smaller sites since April. On paper, it’s a good piece of policy — every planning application now has to leave biodiversity measurably better off than it found it, with a minimum 10% uplift secured for 30 years. You can’t argue with the intent.

The side effect nobody really talks about is what it’s doing to the small ecological consultancies who were already the engine room of this work.

The Shape of the Problem

A single BNG assessment on a modest site now generates a spectacular amount of paperwork. There’s the baseline habitat survey, the Statutory Biodiversity Metric calculation, a condition assessment for every parcel, a post-development design scenario, a habitat management and monitoring plan that has to stretch across three decades, an S106 or conservation covenant draft, evidence submissions, planning authority queries, and a monitoring schedule that triggers every few years for the next thirty.

For a greenfield site of a few hectares, the documentation pack can reach 80 to 120 pages. For an ecologist running a practice of two or three people, that is three to five days of work per project. On top of the fieldwork. On top of the other projects. On top of the planning consultations that come back three weeks later asking for a row of the metric to be redone because the developer changed the footprint by four square metres.

Why It Hits Small Firms Hardest

Large consultancies have enough volume to build internal templates, spread metric specialists across projects, and hand junior staff the report-assembly work. They’ve absorbed BNG into their cost base.

The small independents — the ones typically doing the most careful, most site-specific work — do not have those advantages. A two-person practice cannot dedicate one person to administrative scaffolding without halving its survey capacity. What we see repeatedly is experienced ecologists spending forty to fifty per cent of their billable week writing, formatting, and cross-referencing documents that ninety per cent duplicate the structure of the last one.

The people with the deepest field knowledge end up doing the least fieldwork. That is the quiet crisis.

The Knock-On Effects

Three things follow from this, and none of them are what the policy intended.

Survey quality gets squeezed. When admin eats the week, fieldwork gets compressed into fewer visits, shorter windows, and — in the worst cases — seasonal passes that really ought to span a full year. The ecology itself gets shortchanged by the paperwork that was meant to protect it.

Smaller practices drop out of BNG work. Some are refusing to take on net gain projects altogether because the margins do not survive the overhead. That hands the market to larger firms, which is not bad in itself, but it removes a tier of specialist, relationship-led practice that planning authorities actually rely on.

Planning consultations slow down. Overworked consultants take longer to turn around revisions. Local planning authorities take longer to validate submissions with formatting issues. Developers wait longer for determinations. The policy designed to speed biodiversity recovery is, in practice, making the planning system slower.

What Can Be Done

There are three honest levers.

The first is template discipline. Most of a BNG report is boilerplate that could be written once and reused. A huge amount of what practices produce today is retyped from scratch because their template library is a handful of Word documents scattered across the team’s laptops. Investing a week in proper template infrastructure — including a single source of truth for the metric, standardised condition assessment language, and a shared style for the management and monitoring plan — pays for itself in the first month.

The second is input capture at the point of survey. A surveyor returning from site with a mix of scribbled notes, GPS tracks, and photos still has to transcribe all of it into the metric and into the report. The gap between “data collected” and “data in report” is where most of the lost time lives. Tools that let surveyors structure their observations while they are on site, rather than five days later at a desk, compress that gap significantly.

The third is genuinely automating the writing. The metric, the baseline description, the condition narrative, the management plan structure — these are repetitive, rule-based outputs that do not require a senior ecologist to type them word by word. Clinical judgement should stay with the ecologist. The writing around it does not have to.

The Bit That Matters

None of this is about replacing ecologists. The sector cannot afford to lose the experience that is currently being ground down by admin — it needs to protect it.

BNG is only going to get more demanding. The metric will evolve, habitat condition assessments will get more granular, the monitoring requirements will lengthen. The practices that invest in reducing their per-project overhead now are the ones that will still be taking on this work in five years. The practices that do not will quietly exit the space, and that will be a genuine loss.

If you run a small ecological consultancy and this rings true — we would be interested to hear how the admin burden is actually landing for you. It is the sort of thing we like thinking about.
